Supplier Collaboration Platform · 800+ Vendors
Shahi Exports · Procurement & Logistics · 2023–24
+22% OTIF
−30% Lead Time
+
The Situation
800+ vendors. POs via email. ASN confirmations informal. GRN actuals unused in ERP. Sourcing decisions made on gut and relationship — not performance data. Delivery failures were diagnosed after the fact, never anticipated.
What Most Programs Would Have Done
Deploy SRM module, run big-bang onboarding, declare the portal live, measure by login rates. The scorecard would have gone stale within 90 days. Non-compliant vendors managed by email — exactly as before.
What Was Actually Built
Vendor scorecard auto-generated from ERP GRN data — no manual entry. Onboarding in 3 cohorts with SLA accountability. Vendor success motion built before the portal launched. TnA alert logic designed with operations, not handed down by IT.
"The hardest part wasn't the technology. It was getting 800 suppliers to change how they communicate — and building a success motion that made adoption stick rather than chasing it."
MES Deployment · 24 Manufacturing Plants
Shahi Exports · Production Engineering · 2023–24
+18% Throughput
−50% MTTR
+
The Situation
24 plants. Production tracking on whiteboards and verbal reports. Downtime recorded retrospectively. Line balancing by intuition. The weekly review was always backward-looking, never forward-acting.
What Most Programs Would Have Done
Deploy standard MES configuration simultaneously. Downtime codes defined by IT. WIP logic from vendor templates. Floor supervisors would have found workarounds within the first month.
What Was Actually Built
Downtime codes and WIP tracking co-designed with plant managers. Exception design — what the system does when a supervisor bypasses it under deadline pressure — was answered first, not last. Site champions trained before go-live.
"The factory floor doesn't lie. Every supervisor knows which line has a problem and why. The job was building a system they trusted enough to tell the truth into."
WMS Rollout · 25 Warehouses
Shahi Exports · Supply Chain · 2023
+35% Dock-to-Stock
90% Adoption Rate
+
The Situation
25 warehouses with heterogeneous processes. Inventory accuracy variable. Dock-to-stock times long and inconsistent. A single configuration could not serve all 25 without either oversimplifying or over-engineering.
What Most Programs Would Have Done
Define configuration in a workshop, deploy to all 25 rapidly. Unusual put-away requirements forced into a standard process that didn't fit. Adoption at 60% at best — technically counted as acceptable.
What Was Actually Built
3-warehouse pilot with explicit commitment to change the standard configuration before the next rollout. Workflow iterations from pilot learnings built into the standard before sites 4 through 25 went live.
"A pilot that can't change the configuration is a demonstration, not a pilot. The value is the learning it produces and whether it makes it into the next 22 sites."
LLM-Powered Document Extraction · Finance & Compliance
Shahi Exports · Finance & IT · 2024
−70% Effort
Audit-Ready Accuracy
+
The Situation
Invoice and compliance document processing was manual and error-prone. Documents arrived in multiple formats. Significant hours per week spent on extraction before actual processing could begin. Audit trails incomplete.
What Most Programs Would Have Done
Deploy AI extraction tool, set accuracy threshold based on vendor promise. Edge cases — handwritten annotations, non-standard formats — left to operations to handle manually, with no protocol and no audit trail.
What Was Actually Built
Human-in-the-loop protocol designed before the model went live. Accuracy thresholds set by the compliance team, not the AI vendor. Exception handling was the first thing built, not the last.
"The compliance team's trust depended on knowing the system would tell them when it was uncertain. An AI that never says 'I'm not sure' is an AI that cannot be audited."

Build a comprehensive portal with every data point IT thought relevant. Buyers would have logged in once, found it overwhelming, and continued sending emails.

Order-to-Execution Automation · 8 Departments
Shahi Exports · Operations · 2024
−45% Errors
−70% Manual Effort
+
The Situation
Order processing required sequential handoffs across 8 departments. Each handoff manual, each department with its own format. Errors introduced at any stage compounded downstream — discovered at the point where correction was most expensive.
What Most Programs Would Have Done
Automate the current process as-is. Map, digitize, connect. The errors would have moved faster — not been eliminated.
What Was Actually Built
Error analysis first: where do errors originate and at what stage are they discovered? Requirements from all 6 functional heads before scope was locked. Automation designed around error prevention, not process digitization.
"Automating a broken process makes a broken process faster. Understanding where errors originate determines whether the outcome is 45% fewer errors or 45% faster errors."
Point of View

Three things the industry
gets consistently wrong

Not observations borrowed from a consulting framework — patterns observed from running the programs above. The consistent difference between deployments that changed something and those that declared victory at go-live.

I
⚡
The deployment is not the transformation
A large textile manufacturer spent ₹40 crore on a best-in-class MES. Eighteen months later, 60% of supervisors had reverted to manual tracking during peak season — not because the system failed technically, but because no one designed for the moment when a buyer deadline and a system login compete for the same thirty seconds.

Bain's 2024 research found only 12% of business transformations achieve their original ambitions. The measurement framework of most manufacturing technology programs is built to declare success regardless of what happens on the floor. On-time delivery, training completion — green. OTIF, throughput, yield — unchanged.
88%of transformations fail to meet original ambitions
(Bain & Company, April 2024)
II
📊
India's gap is data discipline, not technology
A mid-sized apparel factory in Tiruppur and a comparable factory in Guangzhou run identical ERP software. The Guangzhou review takes forty minutes — data is clean, current, trusted. The Tiruppur review takes three hours reconciling what the system says against what the supervisor says, because nobody believes either.

China's manufacturing leap was built on upstream behavioral discipline — data entry habitual before anyone asked for analytics. Most AI investment flowing into Indian manufacturing today is not wrong in direction — it is wrong in sequence. The foundation comes first, and most programs skip it.
50%of ERP implementations fail on first attempt
(industry data, widely cited)
III
🔗
60% of delivery risk lives outside the factory
A shipment that misses its date costs 3–15% of order value in penalties and relationship damage. The standard response is to look inward — line efficiency, machine downtime, workforce productivity. The data is more inconvenient than that.

Analysis across large apparel manufacturers points the same direction: late materials, quality rejections at inward inspection, and quantity shortfalls from suppliers account for the majority of shipment delays — not production inefficiency. The factory performs. The supplier ecosystem fails. And visibility into it barely exists until the failure has materialized.
60–70%of delivery risk originates in the supplier ecosystem,
not the factory floor

Research Insight · Adoption Models
China's cluster-based, state-coordinated model cannot be replicated in India's fragmented, family-owned industrial base. The playbook has to be rebuilt from scratch — segmented by readiness, designed for low data maturity, with business outcomes pulling technology adoption rather than mandates pushing it.
Research Insight · The Generation-Skip Trap
Most manufacturers are jumping from paper-based tracking directly to AI-powered dashboards, bypassing the intermediate layer of clean operational data. The result is sophisticated software layered on undisciplined inputs. The dashboards look impressive in a board presentation. On the production floor, the supervisor still uses the whiteboard.
Research Insight · The Mid-Market Gap — The Critical Observation
Of India's approximately 17,000 registered garment manufacturers (Appareify, 2023), a significant mid-market segment sits between ₹50 Cr and ₹500 Cr revenue — too large for SMB tools, too small for enterprise implementations, and too fragmented to attract large technology vendors. This segment will define Indian manufacturing competitiveness by 2030. India's total textile and apparel exports stood at $34.4 billion in FY 2023–24 (Ministry of Textiles), with apparel alone contributing $14.5 billion — a sector whose competitiveness depends on the operational efficiency of mid-market manufacturers who currently lack the tools to achieve it.

Manufacturing Intelligence · B2B SaaS · Apparel
LoomIQ
The question a manufacturing COO actually asks on a Monday morning has never had a system built around it. Until now.
The Observation
Running the weekly production review makes one thing clear: the question that actually matters — "which lines need intervention this week?" — takes two days to answer. An analyst pulls from Movex/M3, joins WFX quality data, reconciles against floor supervisor reports, and by the time it lands in the meeting, the intervention window has closed. The data existed. The intelligence didn't.
The Research Gap
Horizontal BI tools don't know that an efficiency drop on Line 4 plus a fabric shortfall in Category B means a delivery risk three weeks out. That causal chain is domain knowledge, not software capability. No existing tool encodes it. That is the gap — and the moat.
What Was Built
Generic connectors — CSV, REST API, database — not integrations that break when the ERP changes
Factory Pulse: live efficiency, quality, capacity per line — intervention-first, not dashboard-first
Predictive risk engine: delivery risk and quality anomalies flagged before they materialise
Pre-loaded apparel manufacturing intervention playbooks — specific actions, not generic suggestions
Natural language interface: plain English questions, SQL-validated answers with source attribution
Monday morning intelligence brief auto-delivered to WhatsApp or email
"I've run the weekly production review. I know what a COO asks on Monday morning. No existing tool answers it without two days of analyst prep. That gap — between what the software promises and what the floor needs — is the product."
Artifacts Produced
Full BRD v2.0 · Wireframe prototype · Connector framework · KPI definitions with edge cases · Alert logic with 7 lifecycle states · LLM architecture with SQL validation guardrails · Gaps analysis across compliance, mobile, onboarding, ecosystem integrations

LoomIQ ships pre-loaded with causal relationships that matter in apparel manufacturing. Supplier Intelligence Layer
The vendor scorecard that actually gets used — automatic, honest, feeding directly back into sourcing decisions.
The Observation
Enterprise SRM tools technically have scorecard modules. Almost nobody uses them — they require consultant configuration, manual updates, and don't feed back into "who gets the next PO." Most manufacturers track vendor reliability in someone's head or a stale Excel sheet. The data to build a live scorecard already exists in every ERP. The gap is the layer that connects it and makes the output actionable.
The Research Insight
Average lead times hide variance. The signal that predicts supplier reliability is how a vendor communicates when something goes wrong — not their average when everything runs smoothly. Building systems that capture exception behavior is where sourcing intelligence becomes genuinely useful.
What Was Built
Auto-generated vendor scorecard from ERP GRN data — no manual entry, no consultant to maintain
Variance tracking: how vendors behave under pressure is more predictive than their average
TnA alert system: at-risk deliveries flagged before the delay is confirmed
Sourcing recommendation: best vendor by category on live performance, not relationship
3-cohort onboarding model with SLA accountability and vendor success motion
"Vendor scorecard data exists in every ERP. Nobody reads it — extracting it requires a data pull, a join across three tables, and someone to clean it before the Monday meeting. Automating that layer moves OTIF."

A version of this was delivered at Shahi Exports for 800+ vendors across procurement and logistics. The hardest part wasn't the technology — it was building a vendor success motion that made adoption stick.

How the Work Gets Done

Operating principles from programs
that succeeded and programs that didn't

Not frameworks. Observed patterns — the consistent difference between programs that changed something and programs that declared victory at go-live.

01
Sequence before sophistication
The most common failure mode in manufacturing transformation is deploying the right technology at the wrong stage of organizational readiness. Data discipline before analytics. Operational visibility before AI. Getting the sequence right is a more valuable capability than knowing which technology to deploy.
01
02
Scope around the business, not the software
When technology vendors define scope, scope reflects the product's capabilities. When the business problem defines scope, implementations are smaller, faster, and more likely to change something. The discipline is resisting the feature expansion that comes standard with every enterprise software sale.
02
03
Design for the exception, not the happy path
Most systems are built assuming people will use them correctly. On a manufacturing floor under deadline pressure, they won't. Designing the compliant behavior to be easier than the workaround — that's where adoption is actually won or lost.
03
04
Pilot with a genuine feedback loop
A pilot that can't change the standard configuration before rollout is a demonstration, not a pilot. The value is the learning it produces and whether that learning makes it into the next 22 sites. Most programs treat the pilot as a checkbox and wonder why site 15 looks like site 1.
04
05
Measure business outcomes, not project KPIs
A program can go green on every project KPI — budget, timeline, training completion — and have zero impact on OTIF, throughput, or defect rate. Building the measurement framework around business outcomes from day one is the only way to know whether the transformation actually happened.
05
06
Domain knowledge is the moat
Generic tools fail in manufacturing because they don't understand that an efficiency drop on Line 4 plus a material shortfall in fabric category B means a delivery risk three weeks out. Products and programs that encode domain knowledge are defensible in a way that features alone never are.
