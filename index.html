The Product Problem
800+ vendors, zero real-time visibility. Sourcing decisions were being made on relationship and gut — not performance data. The question was whether to buy an enterprise SRM or build a focused intelligence layer on top of existing ERP data. Chose to build — because the data already existed in GRN actuals. Onboarding in 3 cohorts with SLA accountability — not big-bang. Scorecard auto-generated from ERP data, no manual entry. Vendor success motion designed before the portal launched — adoption was the product, not the software. TnA alert logic co-designed with operations, not handed down by IT.

OTIF improved ~22%. Lead times fell ~30%. The scorecard is still updated automatically. The key was designing the vendor success motion before go-live — most SRM implementations die because this is treated as an afterthought.
"The product isn't the portal. The product is the behavior change. The portal just makes the behavior change easier than the old way."
Program Architecture
MES Deployment · 24 Manufacturing Plants
Shahi Exports · Production Engineering · 2023–24
+18% Throughput
−50% MTTR
+
The Program Problem
24 plants, each with its own production tracking habits. Standard MES configuration would have forced a single process onto floors with fundamentally different constraints. The real design challenge: how do you build a system that 24 different plant managers will trust enough to tell the truth into?
How It Was Structured
Downtime codes and WIP logic co-designed with plant managers. Exception design — what the system does when a supervisor bypasses it under deadline pressure — was the first question answered, not the last. Site champions trained before go-live. Adoption built in, not chased after.
The PgM Lesson
A pilot that can't change the standard configuration before rollout is a demonstration, not a pilot. 3-plant pilot with genuine feedback loop, then scaled. Every lesson from the pilot made it into sites 4 through 24. Most programs skip this and wonder why site 15 looks like site 1.
"The factory floor doesn't lie. The job was building a system they trusted enough to tell the truth into — not one that made them feel watched."
Product Discovery
Production Tracking Portal · Global Buyers
Shahi Exports · Merchandising & IT · 2024
−55% Queries
+20% Trust Scores
+
The Discovery
20+ global buyers sending daily status requests. The merchandising team spent significant time answering questions that production data could answer automatically. Classic discovery problem: what are buyers actually asking, versus what IT wanted to build? Conducted buyer friction analysis before any spec was written.
The Scope Decision
The portal was scoped entirely around the 6 questions buyers actually ask most. Nothing else was built. IT wanted 14 features. The product shipped with 6. Buyers had no reason to send a status email — not because they were told to stop, but because the product answered their question faster.
The Outcome
−55% status query volume. +20% buyer trust scores. The lesson: ruthless scoping is a product skill, not a delivery constraint. Every feature you don't build is a feature that can't confuse the user.
"A portal that answers six questions perfectly eliminates more email than a portal that answers fifty questions adequately."
AI Product
LLM-Powered Document Extraction · Finance & Compliance
Shahi Exports · Finance & IT · 2024
−70% Effort
Audit-Ready
+
The AI Product Challenge
Invoice and compliance document processing was manual, error-prone, and across multiple formats. The temptation was to deploy an AI extraction tool and declare success. The real question: how do you build an AI product that a compliance team will trust enough to act on? Trust, not accuracy, was the design constraint.
The Design Decisions
Human-in-the-loop protocol designed before the model went live. Accuracy thresholds set by the compliance team, not the AI vendor. Exception handling was the first thing built, not the last. Every low-confidence extraction flagged for human review with a clear audit trail.
Why It Worked
The system is trustworthy because it knows what it doesn't know. An AI product that never expresses uncertainty cannot be audited — and a compliance team will never fully adopt something they can't audit. Designing for uncertainty was the product insight.
"An AI that never says 'I'm not sure' is an AI that cannot be audited. Designing for uncertainty was the product insight, not the engineering challenge."

LoomIQ
The question a manufacturing COO actually asks on Monday morning has never had a system built around it. Until now.
The Observation
Running weekly production reviews made one thing clear: the question that actually matters — "which lines need intervention this week?" — takes two days to answer. An analyst pulls from the ERP, joins quality data, reconciles against floor supervisor reports. By the time the output lands in the meeting, the intervention window has closed. The data existed. The intelligence didn't.
The Product Gap
Horizontal BI tools don't know that an efficiency drop on Line 4 plus a material shortfall in Category B means a delivery risk three weeks out. That causal chain is domain knowledge, not software capability. No existing tool encodes it. That is the gap — and the moat.
What Was Built
Generic connectors — CSV, REST API, database — not integrations that break when the ERP changes
Factory Pulse: live efficiency, quality, capacity per line — intervention-first, not dashboard-first
Predictive risk engine: delivery risk and quality anomalies flagged before they materialise
Pre-loaded manufacturing intervention playbooks — specific actions, not generic suggestions
Natural language interface: plain English questions, SQL-validated answers
Monday morning intelligence brief auto-delivered to WhatsApp or email
Why It Has Staying Power
LoomIQ ships pre-loaded with causal relationships that matter in manufacturing. That domain knowledge doesn't live in any API — it lives in operations people who've run production reviews for years. Generic tools can add features. They cannot add the floor-level pattern recognition that makes the intelligence layer actually work.
Artifacts Produced
Product specification complete · Wireframe prototype · Connector framework · KPI definitions with edge cases · Alert logic · LLM architecture with SQL validation guardrails
Market Position
India has approximately 17,000 registered garment manufacturers (Appareify, 2023). Priced at ₹50K–2.5L/month — positioned below enterprise analytics and above Excel, in the gap where the real decision-making pain lives.
"I've run the weekly production review. I know what a COO asks on Monday morning. No existing tool answers it without two days of analyst prep. That gap — between what the software promises and what the floor needs — is the product."

Supplier Intelligence Layer
The vendor scorecard that actually gets used — automatic, honest, feeding directly back into sourcing decisions.
The Observation
Enterprise SRM tools technically have scorecard modules. Almost nobody uses them — they require consultant configuration, manual updates, and don't feed back into "who gets the next PO." Most manufacturers track vendor reliability in someone's head or a stale Excel sheet. The data to build a live scorecard already exists in every ERP — GRN actuals against PO promises. The gap is the layer that connects them.
The Key Research Insight
Average lead times hide variance. The signal that predicts supplier reliability is how a vendor communicates when something goes wrong — not their average when everything runs smoothly. Building systems that capture exception behavior is where sourcing intelligence becomes genuinely useful.
What Was Built
Auto-generated vendor scorecard from ERP GRN data — no manual entry, no consultant to maintain
Variance tracking: how vendors behave under pressure is more predictive than their average
TnA alert system: at-risk deliveries flagged before the delay is confirmed
Sourcing recommendation: best vendor by category on live performance, not relationship
3-cohort onboarding model with SLA accountability and vendor success motion
Delivered Outcomes
This was delivered at Shahi Exports for 800+ vendors. OTIF improved ~22%. Lead times fell ~30%. The hardest part wasn't the technology — it was building a vendor success motion that made adoption stick.
The Broader Thesis
60–70% of delivery risk originates outside the factory. The tools for internal visibility are mature. Supplier-side intelligence is the most underserved layer in the entire manufacturing stack — and the highest ROI for any manufacturer serious about on-time delivery.
"Vendor scorecard data exists in every ERP. Nobody reads it — extracting it requires a data pull, a join across three tables, and someone to clean it before the Monday meeting. Automating that layer moves OTIF."

SMB Operations · India · Side Projects
SMB Operations Suite
Three products built from the same observation: the tools that exist at the top of the manufacturing value chain don't exist for the businesses at the bottom of it.
VendorLedger
A vendor payment OS for Indian SMBs. The daily question "did we pay the packaging supplier?" takes 30 minutes to answer and still sometimes gets wrong. Accounting software is too complex. Khatabook is a ledger, not a payment orchestration tool. VendorLedger owns the gap between those two.
QuoteKit
Fast, professional quoting for custom-order small businesses. The RFQ process is solved at enterprise scale. For the printing shop, fabricator, or caterer — it's a calculator, a Word document, and mental math. QuoteKit answers in under 2 minutes what used to take two hours.
OrderFlow
An operations layer for businesses that run on WhatsApp. Dukaan built the storefront. Khatabook built the ledger. Nobody built the operations layer behind the storefront — what's ordered, in production, pending payment, needs follow-up. OrderFlow owns that unoccupied territory.
Why These Exist
At Shahi, 800+ vendors are coordinated through structured systems and real-time visibility. The small units on the other end manage the same coordination on a phone with no system at all. That asymmetry is the market. The manufacturing background makes the product design accurate for people who actually need it.
"Enterprise has Ariba. The 10-person business supplying to enterprises has a notebook. That gap is enormous, completely underserved, and directly in the path of India's formalization story."

Point of View

Three things product leaders
get wrong in complex environments

Not borrowed from a framework — patterns observed from running programs that succeeded, programs that didn't, and the consistent difference between both.

I
⚡
The deployment is not the transformation
A large manufacturer spent ₹40 crore on a best-in-class MES. Eighteen months later, 60% of supervisors had reverted to manual tracking during peak season — not because the system failed technically, but because no one designed for the moment when a buyer deadline and a system login compete for the same thirty seconds.

Bain's 2024 research found only 12% of business transformations achieve their original ambitions. The measurement framework of most technology programs is built to declare success at launch, regardless of what happens six months later when the team has moved on.
88%of transformations fail to meet original ambitions (Bain & Company, April 2024)
II
🔍
Real discovery happens where the work happens
The best product insights in manufacturing don't come from user interviews — they come from the floor. A mid-sized manufacturing unit in Tiruppur and a comparable unit in Guangzhou run identical ERP software. The Guangzhou review takes forty minutes — data is clean, current, trusted. The Tiruppur review takes three hours reconciling what the system says against what the supervisor says.

The technology is identical. The data discipline is not. You only discover this by being on the floor, not by reading a usage dashboard.
50%of ERP implementations fail on first attempt (industry data, widely cited)
III
🎯
The best scope decision is usually what you don't build
When building the buyer tracking portal at Shahi, IT wanted 14 features. The product shipped with 6 — the 6 questions buyers actually asked most. Status query volume dropped 55%. Buyer trust scores rose 20%.

Ruthless scoping is a product skill, not a delivery constraint. Every feature you don't build is a feature that can't confuse the user, can't break in production, and can't dilute the core value proposition. The discipline of saying no — especially to your own team — is what separates good products from bloated ones.

Global Context

Where India sits — and why it matters for product

India's manufacturing transformation is a sequencing problem, not a technology problem
Practitioner's View
The Gap
Large manufacturers have ERP and PLM. The mid-market is 5–7 years behind. Analytics tools are deployed but underutilised because the upstream data is undisciplined. Most AI investment is premature — not wrong in direction, wrong in sequence.
The China Comparison
China's manufacturing leap was built on upstream behavioral discipline — data entry habitual before anyone asked for analytics. India deploys the same software and gets dashboards nobody trusts. The data discipline gap is a product design opportunity, not just an ops problem.
The Product Opportunity
The mid-market segment — manufacturers between ₹50 Cr and ₹500 Cr — is too large for SMB tools, too small for enterprise implementations. India's $34.4 billion textile and apparel export sector (Ministry of Textiles, FY24) depends on this segment operating at a level of efficiency it currently cannot achieve.
How I Work

Operating principles

Distilled from programs that worked and programs that didn't — the consistent difference between them.

01
Sequence before sophistication
The most common failure mode is deploying the right technology at the wrong stage of organizational readiness. Getting the sequence right — what comes first, what has to wait — is more valuable than knowing which technology to deploy.
01
02
Scope around the problem, not the software
When technology vendors define scope, scope reflects the product's capabilities. The discipline is resisting feature expansion that comes standard with every enterprise software sale — and every internal stakeholder request.
02
03
Design for the exception, not the happy path
Most products are built assuming people will use them correctly. Under deadline pressure, they won't. Designing the intended behavior to be easier than the workaround — that's where adoption is actually won or lost.
03
04
Discovery happens where the work happens
The best product insights don't come from user interviews alone — they come from being in the environment where the problem exists. Floor-level observation surfaces things that no survey or analytics dashboard will ever reveal.
04
05
Measure outcomes, not outputs
A program can go green on every delivery KPI and have zero impact on the metric that actually matters. Building measurement frameworks around business outcomes from day one is the only way to know whether the product actually worked.
05
06
Domain knowledge compounds
Generic tools fail in complex environments because they don't understand the causal chains that matter in that domain. Products that encode domain knowledge — and teams that have it — are defensible in a way that features alone never are.
